The first real read on the iPhone 18 Pro is in, and it isn't great. Apple has told some of its suppliers to cut production of components for the iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone 18 Pro Max because demand has come in softer than planned, [Nikkei Asia reported](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/technology/exclusive-apple-cuts-iphone-18-pro-orders-due-to-soft-demand) on Friday.

According to Nikkei's sources, Apple has been more cautious about shipments since early September, and component orders for October are down at least 15% from what Apple originally asked for. One executive-level source put the October cut at 15% to 20% for both Pro models and said nobody yet knows what happens after that, [per 9to5Mac's summary](https://9to5mac.com/2026/10/09/report-apple-cuts-iphone-18-pro-production-due-to-soft-demand/). Because suppliers work on different lead times, the cut hits some of them this month and not others. Whether Apple trims further from November on is still an open question.

This is a supply chain report, not a sales number. But it lines up with everything else we've seen this fall.

## The price is doing what you'd expect

The iPhone 18 Pro starts at $1,199 and the Pro Max at $1,299, [$100 more than the models they replace](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/09/apple-cuts-iphone-18-pro-orders-price-demand/). Apple has said openly that the increases come from AI-driven memory chip costs. Data center buildouts are soaking up DRAM and flash, and the same squeeze has already pushed up prices on Macs, iPads and even older iPhones.

What buyers got for that extra $100 is part of the problem. The 18 Pro models ship with 12GB of RAM, the same as the iPhone 17 Pro, and one rumor says Apple originally planned 16GB before costs got in the way. So the phone costs more partly because memory got expensive, and it doesn't come with more memory. That's a hard upgrade pitch for anyone who bought a 17 Pro last year.

## The split launch is a second factor

Nikkei also points to Apple's new release calendar. This year, for the first time, Apple held the regular iPhone back from its fall launch. The regular iPhone 18 is expected next spring, alongside the cheaper iPhone 18e and the thin iPhone Air 2. One supply chain manager told Nikkei that demand is weaker than in years when every new model launched together.

That makes sense. In a normal year, a lot of people walk into a store for the base model and walk out with a Pro. This year there's no base model to compare against, and anyone who only wanted a new iPhone, not a Pro iPhone, has a good reason to wait until spring. Some of this softness may just be demand moving to the first half of 2027 rather than disappearing.

## Why it may not show up in revenue right away

Fewer units at a higher price can still mean steady revenue. Nikkei notes that the price increase means lower demand won't immediately translate into lower revenue for Apple. If Apple sells somewhat fewer Pros at $100 more each, the dollar total can hold up even as unit counts slip.

The Mac business is a preview of how this plays out. IDC estimates Apple shipped 5.9 million Macs in the third quarter, down from 6.7 million a year earlier, [according to MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/09/mac-shipments-fall-but-apple-gains-ground/). That was still enough for Apple's share to rise from 8.5% to 9.5%, because the overall PC market fell 20.1%. IDC blamed the same mix of memory shortages, higher prices and buyers who stocked up early to get ahead of increases. Apple is losing volume, just more slowly than most of the market.

## What to watch

Apple reports its fiscal fourth-quarter results in early November, which will cover the first few weeks of iPhone 18 Pro sales. Apple doesn't break out unit sales, so watch whether iPhone revenue holds up and what the company says about memory costs for the December quarter. The other tell is discounting: if carriers and retailers start leaning on iPhone 18 Pro deals before the holidays, that's the market confirming what Nikkei's sources are describing.
