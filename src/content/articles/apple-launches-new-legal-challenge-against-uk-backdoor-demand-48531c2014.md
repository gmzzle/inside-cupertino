Apple filed another legal challenge against the UK government last month, this time fighting a narrower—but still lousy—demand for backdoor access to encrypted iCloud backups. [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/08/03/apple-legal-challenge-against-uk-demand/) reported the complaint went to the Investigatory Powers Tribunal in July.

Here's what happened: the Home Office originally wanted Apple to build a backdoor covering both UK and U.S. customers. That blew up into a diplomatic mess when Washington objected, so the UK backed down. Then last year they just reissued the demand with a smaller scope—UK users only. Same technical capability notice, different geographic boundary. Apple's position hasn't changed: building any backdoor weakens encryption for everyone, and limiting it by country code doesn't fix the problem.

The UK government leans on the Investigatory Powers Act to justify these notices, arguing they're essential for investigating terrorism and child abuse. That's the same framing governments always use, and it's effective politically. But the technical reality is that you can't create a secure backdoor that only good guys can use. Either the encryption works for everyone or it's compromised for everyone. Apple's been consistent on this since the San Bernardino case in 2016, and they're clearly not folding now just because the demand got geographically smaller.

This is Apple's second active case against these capability notices at the IPT. The first one is scheduled for December. That's unusual—companies usually fight one case at a time and wait for resolution. Filing a second challenge suggests Apple sees these demands as an ongoing problem, not a one-off dispute they can settle quietly.

The UK isn't alone in pushing for this kind of access, but they're one of the few governments willing to use legal force rather than just asking nicely. If Apple loses and has to comply, the precedent gets cited everywhere from Australia to India to Brazil. That's why they're spending the legal fees to fight it twice.
