For most of the iPhone era, Apple was the buyer every flash memory maker had to please, and it knew it. A new report from The Wall Street Journal's Rolfe Winkler lays out how hard Apple leaned on that position, and why the tables have turned: AI data centers now buy far more flash than Apple does, and the suppliers it once pushed around are setting the terms.

That shift isn't just industry gossip. It's a big part of why so many Apple prices went up this year.

## How Apple used to play it

According to the Journal's reporting, as summarized by [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/30/apple-flash-storage-suppliers-revenge-report/) and [Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/apples-procurement-team-once-threatened-to-have-sandisk-ceo-crawl-on-his-knees-audited-memory-suppliers-books-so-often-it-got-a-volume-discount-with-deloitte/):

- **Refused deliveries.** Apple would order flash at very low prices, then sometimes refuse delivery if market prices fell, leaving suppliers holding unsold chips, including parts made specifically for Apple.
- **Opened the books.** Apple required "most favored customer" terms and audited suppliers to make sure nobody else was getting a better price.
- **Played hardball.** The report says former procurement chief Tony Blevins threatened a Sandisk salesperson around the end of 2015, after Sandisk declined to send Apple extra supply because other customers were paying more.

Apple's response to the Journal was brief: a spokesperson said the company deeply values its decades-long supplier partnerships.

## What changed

Scale. Citing estimates from research firm TechInsights, the report says that two years ago Apple bought nearly twice as much flash storage as Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Google combined. Now those hyperscalers together buy about double Apple's share.

When you're no longer the biggest customer in the room, most-favored-customer clauses and refused deliveries stop working. Suppliers have other buyers lined up, and SK Hynix said on its earnings call this week that it expects the memory shortage to last through 2030.

## Where you feel it

Apple has raised prices on more than a dozen products this year, including iPhones, iPads, Macs, HomePods and Apple TV, and it has pointed to the memory shortage as the reason. The iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max each went up $100 in the U.S., to $1,199 and $1,299.

The squeeze also reaches Apple's famously profitable storage upgrades. TechInsights estimates Apple's gross profit on a $200 jump from 256GB to 512GB on the iPhone 18 Pro is about $134, down from roughly $185 on the equivalent iPhone 17 Pro upgrade last year. That's still a healthy margin, but it's a meaningful drop on one of Apple's easiest money-makers.

## Our take

There's some poetic justice in this story, and the suppliers clearly enjoy telling it. But the more useful takeaway for Apple buyers is the timeline. If memory makers are right that tight supply runs to 2030, the price bumps of 2026 may not be a one-off, and "just wait for prices to come back down" may not be a great plan for anyone eyeing a higher-storage iPhone or Mac.

It also makes this week more interesting. Apple reports fiscal fourth-quarter earnings soon, and how much of the memory cost it's absorbing versus passing on will be worth listening for on the call.

## What to watch

- **Apple's earnings call** for any comments on memory costs and gross margin.
- **Long-term supply deals.** Reports suggest Apple has been locking in flash supply by contract rather than chasing the lowest spot price.
- **Storage-tier pricing** on the next round of Macs and iPads, where Apple's upgrade pricing is most exposed.
