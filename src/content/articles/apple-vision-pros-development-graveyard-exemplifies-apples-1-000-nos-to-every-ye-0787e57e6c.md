[AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/26/09/27/apple-vision-pros-development-graveyard-exemplifies-apples-1000-nos-to-every-yes?utm_source=rss) is pushing back on the weird cottage industry that's emerged around treating Vision Pro like a case study in corporate failure. The premise: every time a Vision Pro engineer leaves Apple or a prototype feature gets killed, there's a new round of hot takes about how the product is doomed and Apple has lost its way.

The problem is that this completely inverts how Apple has always worked. The graveyard of killed features isn't a bug - it's the entire system. Apple's operational MO for decades has been saying no to almost everything so the few yeses can be genuinely excellent. They built an entire brand on restraint. Remember when the first iPhone launched without copy-paste, MMS, or third-party apps? The feature list was absurdly limited compared to competitors, and it still rewired the entire industry.

Vision Pro gets held to this bizarre standard where shipping a first-gen $3,499 spatial computer that actually works is somehow less impressive than Meta lighting $40 billion on fire for a metaverse no one asked for. The expectation seems to be that Apple should have shipped half-baked AR glasses in 2019, iterated in public like a startup, and called every pivot a "learning moment." That's just not the playbook.

The real tell is how the criticism frame reveals what people want Apple to be versus what Apple actually is. They want rapid iteration, public betas of wild ideas, feature dumps, quarterly pivots based on whatever's trending. Basically, they want Google circa 2012. But Apple's value has always been in the editing - in the discipline to kill good ideas because they're not great ideas, to let products mature in the lab until they're ready for civilian use, and to resist the urge to ship just because competitors are shipping.

Vision Pro's development graveyard isn't evidence of dysfunction - it's evidence the process is working exactly as designed, just on a product where people expected something different.
