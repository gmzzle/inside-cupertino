[9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/10/06/apple-killed-the-mac-minis-best-hidden-feature-video/) discovered that Apple killed the removable SSD module in the new M6 Mac mini. The M4 generation had two accessible NAND slots on the logic board—you could pop the bottom off and swap in bigger storage yourself. Not anymore.

This wasn't some sanctioned upgrade path, but it worked. Enthusiasts bought the base 256GB model and dropped in third-party or Apple-pulled modules for a fraction of what Apple charges for storage tiers. The company sells a 2TB upgrade for $600 over the base model; equivalent SSDs cost maybe $150 retail. Apple was leaving real money on the table, and now they've closed that door.

The new Mac mini still looks identical from the outside, same compact aluminum body. But the storage is now soldered directly to the M6 system-on-chip package, just like the MacBook Air. No modules. No slots. Whatever you buy is what you keep. This aligns the mini with the rest of Apple's lineup—the iMac, Mac Studio, and laptops all have non-upgradeable storage—but it stings because the mini was the last holdout where tinkerers had an option.

Apple's likely rationale: fewer SKU complications, tighter supply chain, and capturing the full margin on storage upgrades. From their perspective, the removable modules were a liability. Customers doing self-upgrades means lost revenue and potential support headaches if someone installs a bad module. From a user perspective, it's a step backward for longevity. A machine you could stretch to 4TB for cheap is now stuck at whatever you paid for upfront.

The M6 mini is still a solid machine—same $599 starting price, faster chip, better efficiency. But if you need more than 512GB, you're now paying Apple's tax or living off external drives.
