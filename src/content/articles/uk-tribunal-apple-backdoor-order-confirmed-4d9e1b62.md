For almost two years, the British government has refused to say whether it ordered Apple to break its own encryption. Everyone following the story knew it had. As of Thursday, the government can't keep pretending otherwise.

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal [ruled](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/OPEN-Judgment-Apple-Inc.-and-Privacy-Int.-Ors.pdf) that the Home Office failed to show that confirming the order would damage national security. The order is a "technical capability notice" (TCN), the tool UK law uses to make companies keep data accessible to security services. The tribunal's judgment says outright that the Home Secretary "has given Apple a technical capability notice."

## Why the secrecy argument fell apart

The government's defense rested on its usual "neither confirm nor deny" policy. The tribunal found that policy had already been broken many times over. As [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/19/uk-loses-secrecy-fight-over-apple-backdoor-order/) summarized the judgment, briefings to the press, including the Financial Times, and talks with U.S. officials meant anyone paying attention already knew Apple had received a TCN. The tribunal called the idea that confirming it would tip off bad actors "fanciful."

[Privacy International](https://privacyinternational.org/news-analysis/5896/win-tribunal-orders-uk-government-come-clean-about-secret-order-apple/), which brought a separate challenge with Liberty and two individuals, also pointed out that the notices had been openly discussed by President Trump and his former Director of National Intelligence.

The government isn't backing down on the substance. A spokesperson told [Reuters](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/uk-tribunal-confirms-government-order-demanding-access-apple-data-6565996) that the notice "simply requires that they maintain the ability" to help police and intelligence agencies.

## What this does and doesn't decide

This ruling does **not** say the order is illegal. It only settles whether the order's existence can be acknowledged. The contents of the notice are still secret, and the tribunal also issued private judgments it says can't be published.

What it changes is where the real fight happens. Because the TCN's existence is no longer a secret, the tribunal says much of the main hearing can take place in open court. That includes Apple's arguments on:

- whether UK law even lets the government regulate the security features a tech company offers its customers,
- whether the government's consultation process was proper, and
- whether the order is a disproportionate interference with privacy rights under the European Convention on Human Rights.

The first two are pure questions of law and can be argued entirely in public, according to the judgment. The parties told the tribunal that a December 2026 hearing isn't practical. They're aiming for the first half of 2027 instead.

## Where this leaves iPhone users in the UK

Nothing changes on your phone today. Apple pulled Advanced Data Protection (ADP) for new UK users in February 2025 because of this fight, and it still isn't available.

Here's the current state, per [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/19/uk-loses-secrecy-fight-over-apple-backdoor-order/):

- **If you never turned on ADP in the UK,** you can't turn it on now. Your iCloud backups, Photos, and Notes use standard protection, which means Apple holds the keys.
- **If you turned it on before the pullback,** it can stay on. Apple says it can't switch it off for you, and it's promised a window to disable it yourself, but there's still no deadline.
- **Some things stay end-to-end encrypted regardless,** including iMessage, FaceTime, passwords, and Health data.

## The bigger picture

This is the second round of this fight. As we [covered in August](/articles/apple-launches-new-legal-challenge-against-uk-backdoor-demand-48531c2014/), the Home Office dropped an earlier demand that covered both UK and U.S. users after Washington objected, then issued a narrower notice believed to focus on UK users. Apple filed a new challenge in July.

Thursday's win is procedural, but it matters. Encryption backdoor orders are built to stay secret. Making the government argue this one in public is the closest thing to transparency anyone has gotten, and the next round will decide whether a country can make Apple weaken security for its own citizens.
