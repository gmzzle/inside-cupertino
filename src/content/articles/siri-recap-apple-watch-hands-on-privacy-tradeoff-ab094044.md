Apple wants your Apple Watch to remember conversations for you. The first two in-depth reviews of the feature landed this week, and both reach the same verdict: Apple got the privacy part right, and that's exactly why the feature isn't very useful yet.

The feature is **Siri Recap**, and it's in the watchOS 27.3 beta now. Here's what it does, what testers found, and whether you should turn it on when it ships.

## What Siri Recap actually does

According to [Apple's support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/148354), Siri Recap "ambiently takes notes on your conversations throughout your day" and turns them into short Apple Intelligence summaries you can look at later.

The key rules:

- **It's opt-in.** It's off by default. You turn it on in the Watch app on your iPhone, and you can set it to run only at certain times or places.
- **No audio is kept.** Apple says the audio is processed and then thrown away. You get a summary, not a recording or a transcript.
- **Summaries can expire.** You can have recaps delete themselves after up to seven days unless you save them.
- **They're encrypted.** Recaps synced through iCloud are end-to-end encrypted, so only you can read them on your devices.
- **It's limited to new hardware.** It needs an Apple Watch Series 12 or Ultra 4 paired with an iPhone that supports Apple Intelligence. It's English-only to start, not available in the EU at launch, and not available to anyone under 18.

MacRumors says the public release is expected with watchOS 27.3 in December.

## What testers found

[MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/20/siri-recap-hands-on/) and [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1037100/apple-siri-recap-audio-intelligence-hands-on) each spent about two weeks with the beta. Their problems overlap almost perfectly.

**It can't tell a conversation from a video.** Both reviewers got recaps of TikToks, Instagram Reels, or YouTube clips playing nearby, written as if they had been real conversations. The Verge notes that Recap turns off when you're wearing headphones, which fixes this, but only if you change your habits for Siri's sake.

**No speaker labels means mixed-up facts.** Because Recap doesn't say who said what, details from different people get blended together. MacRumors described a vet visit where the vet's story about her own pet came out in the summary as if it were the writer's pet. Apple leaves out speaker labels on purpose, so this probably won't get fixed.

**It decides what matters, and you can't check.** Recap leans hard on facts, dates, and numbers. Both reviewers said it got appointment dates right but dropped the parts they actually cared about. Since no audio is kept, there's no way to go back and see what it skipped. Apple's own fine print says summaries "may be incomplete or inaccurate."

**The people around you don't know.** Both reviewers pointed out there's no outward light or sound to tell others that your watch is taking notes. Apple's watch notifies *you* when a scheduled Recap starts or stops, but nobody else gets a signal. Telling them is up to you.

The Verge summed up the tension: "The reality is that better privacy often means a less useful AI product." MacRumors was blunter, calling Siri Recap a feature it isn't going to use and wouldn't recommend.

## Should you use it?

This is beta software, and some of these problems, like summarizing videos or merging unrelated topics, could improve before release. Others, like the missing speaker labels and the lack of audio to double-check, are built into how Apple designed it to protect privacy.

If you get a Series 12 or Ultra 4 and want to try it, here's a sensible way to start:

1. **Use a schedule, not always-on.** Limit it to meetings or appointments where notes would actually help.
2. **Treat recaps as a memory jog, not a record.** For anything important, like medical, legal, or money conversations, write your own notes or confirm details with the other person.
3. **Tell people.** If your watch is listening, say so. It's the decent thing to do, and in some places it may matter legally.
4. **Wear headphones for media.** That keeps your podcasts and videos out of your recaps.

Apple is betting that a privacy-first version of always-listening AI can win over people who would never wear a recording gadget. Right now the privacy holds up. Being useful is the part Apple still has to prove.
