On Friday, October 30, Apple sent a fresh round of threat notifications to iPhone users it believes were individually targeted by mercenary spyware. According to the alert text reported by [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/apple-spyware-notifications-pall-mall-process/), this wave went to "targeted users in 99 countries," and Apple has now notified people in more than 150 countries since the program began in 2021.

It's the second big wave in about eleven weeks. Apple's previous batch, in August, reached users in 110 countries.

Most people will never get one of these alerts. But if you do, or if someone you know does, it helps to know what it means, how to check it's genuine, and what to do next.

## What Apple said, and what it didn't

The notification text, as quoted by CyberScoop, says: "The extreme cost, sophistication, and worldwide nature of mercenary spyware attacks make them some of the most advanced digital threats in existence today. As a result, Apple does not attribute the attacks or the notice you're receiving to any specific attackers or geographical regions."

So there's a lot we don't know:

- **Who's behind it.** Apple never names attackers, governments or spyware vendors.
- **How many people got it.** Apple gives a country count, not a number of recipients. Ninety-nine countries could mean dozens of people or thousands.
- **Which countries.** Apple doesn't publish the list.

CyberScoop reports that recipients include journalists and documentary filmmakers. That fits Apple's own description on its [threat notification support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102174): these attacks cost millions of dollars, target "a very small number of specific individuals," and are aimed at people "likely because of who they are or what they do."

## How to tell if an alert is real

Fake security alerts are a classic phishing trick, so this part matters. According to Apple's support page, a genuine threat notification shows up in a few places:

- An **Apple Threat Notification alert** on your iPhone's Lock Screen and in Settings.
- An **email** to the addresses on your Apple Account, sent from threat-notifications@email.apple.com.
- A **banner at the top of your Apple Account page** when you sign in at account.apple.com.

The simplest check: open a browser yourself, go to account.apple.com, and sign in. If Apple sent you a notification, the banner is there.

Apple is also explicit about what a real alert will never do. It will never ask you to click a link, open a file, install an app or profile, or hand over your Apple Account password or a verification code by email or phone. Any "spyware alert" that asks for one of those is the scam.

## What to do if you get one

Apple's guidance, which Citizen Lab senior researcher John Scott-Railton seconded to CyberScoop, comes down to a few steps:

1. **Turn on Lockdown Mode** in Settings, under Privacy & Security. It's an optional, extreme protection that sharply limits message attachments, some web technologies and other features that spyware tends to exploit. You can turn it off later.
2. **Update your iPhone** to the latest version of iOS.
3. **Get expert help.** Apple points recipients to the free, 24/7 Digital Security Helpline run by the nonprofit Access Now.

Scott-Railton also warned people to be cautious about companies selling paid fixes for spyware infections. And he cautioned that not getting a notification doesn't prove you weren't targeted. Apple only sends these when it has high confidence, and its own support page says its investigations "can never achieve absolute certainty."

## Why this wave lands now

CyberScoop notes the timing: the notifications arrive shortly before final negotiations on industry guidelines for commercial hacking tools under the Pall Mall Process, a voluntary international effort to rein in the spyware trade.

"The number of Apple threat notifications we are seeing from countries around the world indicates that the fight against the scourge of spyware abuse is far from over," Natalia Krapiva, senior tech-legal counsel at Access Now, told CyberScoop.

## Our take

Apple's threat notifications are one of the few ways anyone outside an intelligence agency finds out they've been targeted, and two big waves in under three months suggests the spyware business isn't slowing down. For the vast majority of iPhone owners, the practical lesson is simpler: keep your phone updated, and treat any "security alert" that asks you to click, install or sign in through a link as fake until you've checked account.apple.com yourself.

If you're a journalist, activist, lawyer or anyone whose work makes you a plausible target, Lockdown Mode is worth a serious look now, not after an alert shows up.
