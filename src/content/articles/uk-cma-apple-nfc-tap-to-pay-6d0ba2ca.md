Two years after Apple opened the iPhone's contactless chip to outside apps in the UK, not one company has used it to launch a wallet that competes with Apple Pay. The UK's competition regulator thinks it knows why, and this morning it proposed a fix: make Apple offer more ways in, and make it charge a lot less for the door.

The **Competition and Markets Authority** published the proposal on **Tuesday, October 20**, as a formal [consultation](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/proposed-nfc-access-conduct-requirement-for-apples-mobile-platform) on what it calls an NFC access "conduct requirement" for Apple's mobile platform. The [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cqm26zzl0k12o) and [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/10/20/apple-still-makes-it-too-hard-for-apple-pay-competitors-to-emerge-says-uk/) both picked it up within hours, and Apple has already said it disagrees.

## What the CMA is proposing

There are two standard ways a phone app can do tap-to-pay. A **secure element** (SE) keeps card data on a dedicated chip inside the phone. **Host card emulation** (HCE) keeps the sensitive data off the device and sends the phone short-lived, one-time tokens for each transaction. Neither was invented by Apple, and Android phones support both.

In the UK, Apple only offers the secure element route, through the "NFC & SE Platform" it rolled out in October 2024. The CMA wants two changes:

- **Both standards, not one.** Apple would have to give developers access through HCE as well as SE. The regulator says the evidence it has seen suggests "neither technology raises inherent security concerns," and that HCE support would let UK firms roll out payment services they already offer in the EU.
- **Much cheaper access.** The CMA isn't setting a price. Instead it's proposing principles for how Apple sets one, on "fair, reasonable, transparent and non-discriminatory terms," and says it expects the result to be charges "substantially lower" than Apple's current fees, while still paying Apple fairly for the access.

The power to do this comes from the UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. The CMA has already given Apple's mobile platform "Strategic Market Status" under that law, which is what lets it write rules like this one.

## Why the regulator thinks the current setup isn't working

The CMA's case rests on a simple comparison. In the EU, where Apple settled with the European Commission in 2024 and opened NFC payments through HCE with no fee, alternative wallets appeared fast. The CMA says that within six months, **Vipps** launched the first alternative payment app, followed by **PayPal, Klarna, Curve and BBVA**. In the UK, it says, "not a single firm has launched a wallet-type product to compete with Apple's."

The 147-page consultation document notes that Mastercard and Visa have both announced services to help developers use Apple's UK platform, but says there's "still no evidence of NFC apps launching in the UK" on it. Businesses told the regulator the fees and terms may be too onerous to make a product viable, and that having different technical rules in different countries makes it harder to scale.

The stakes are large in the UK specifically. Citing UK Finance, the CMA says contactless now makes up **39%** of all payments, up from 3% a decade ago. Citing Which?, it says **62%** of UK adults use a digital wallet and Apple Wallet is the most popular, used by half of adults.

CMA chief executive **Sarah Cardell** said the regulator had "heard that high fees, strict terms, and technical restrictions relating to Apple's contactless technology make it too difficult for challengers to innovate and compete." The UK's Financial Conduct Authority backed the move, with its executive director **David Geale** saying it should also "support the expansion of open banking, enabling more people to make payments directly from their bank accounts."

## Apple's answer

Apple isn't on board. In a statement to the BBC, it said its current setup already lets developers in the UK and elsewhere "securely offer contactless transactions directly within their apps," from in-store payments and transit passes to car keys and event tickets. Its bottom line: "We do not believe the CMA should require Apple to adopt an approach with lower privacy and security protections. We will continue to engage constructively to reach an outcome that is in the best interest of our users."

Apple also told the BBC that the EU apps the CMA points to could have launched before the EU changes, which is a direct challenge to the regulator's main piece of evidence.

## What this means

- **Nothing changes on your iPhone yet.** This is a proposal. The consultation closes at **11:59 p.m. UK time on November 17**, and the CMA will decide after reviewing responses.
- **If it sticks, UK iPhone owners could get real Apple Pay rivals.** The CMA lists the kinds of products it expects: other wallets, bank-to-bank payment apps, digital IDs and age checks, car, hotel and office keys, tickets and travel passes, and loyalty apps.
- **The fee piece matters more than the tech piece.** Requiring HCE gives developers a familiar route. But the CMA's own evidence is that Apple's UK platform already exists and nobody built on it. Whether that changes depends on how low "substantially lower" turns out to be.
- **Developers can speak up without breaking their NDAs.** The CMA says non-disclosure agreements with Apple shouldn't stop anyone from responding, and that Apple has confirmed it won't use confidentiality clauses to stop them.
- **Expect this to be the template.** Apple has now lost or settled this fight in the EU and faces it again in the UK. As 9to5Mac points out, most people never change defaults anyway, so a more open tap-to-pay chip may cost Apple less than the fight does.
