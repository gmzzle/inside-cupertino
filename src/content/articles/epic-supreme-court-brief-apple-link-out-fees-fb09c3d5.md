While everyone was unboxing HomeViews on Friday, the most consequential App Store story of the winter quietly moved forward. Epic Games filed its [response brief](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-1311/432659/20261113133444331_25-1311bs.pdf) at the U.S. Supreme Court, asking the justices to keep the contempt ruling that forced Apple to let U.S. developers link to their own web checkouts without paying Apple a cut.

Oral argument is set for **January 11, 2027**. Here's what Epic argued, what the Court is actually deciding, and why it matters if you buy anything inside an iPhone app.

## How we got here, in four steps

1. **2021:** Apple mostly won its antitrust fight with Epic, but Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers ordered it to stop blocking developers from pointing users to outside payment options. [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/09/14/apple-supreme-court-contempt-ruling/) notes that injunction was just 75 words long.
2. **Apple complied, sort of.** It allowed links but charged a 12% to 27% commission on those purchases and limited how the links could look. After payment-processing costs, developers saved little or nothing, and [few signed up](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/05/21/apple-supreme-court-epic-games-case/).
3. **April 2025:** Gonzalez Rogers found Apple in contempt and barred it from collecting any fee on linked-out purchases in the U.S. App Store. Apple hasn't collected on those links since.
4. **The appeal:** The Ninth Circuit upheld the contempt finding but said a total fee ban went too far and sent the case back to set a "reasonable" fee. Apple took it to the Supreme Court, which agreed in June to hear one narrow question.

## The question the justices will answer

Can a court hold a company in contempt for breaking the *spirit* of an injunction, or only its exact words? Apple's [September brief](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/09/14/apple-supreme-court-contempt-ruling/) says the original order never mentioned commissions, so charging one couldn't be contempt.

Epic's answer, per the filing quoted by [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/13/epic-formally-asks-scotus-to-uphold-contempt-ruling-against-apple/), is that Apple's standard would "reduce injunctions to a never-ending game of whack-a-mole." Ban one anti-steering rule, and a company could simply invent a new one that does the same job.

## Epic's sharpest points

- **The math was the point.** Epic says Apple picked 27% knowing that, once developers paid their own processing costs of more than 3%, a linked-out sale would cost them more than a regular in-app purchase at 30%. The brief quotes an Apple executive conceding exactly that.
- **A "sham" justification.** Epic says Apple commissioned an economic report that offered an "entirely manufactured" rationale for the 27% rate.
- **The cover-up findings.** The brief points to the district court's findings that Apple claimed privilege over more than 57,000 documents and that its vice president of finance "outright lied under oath."

Epic also gives the Court a fallback. If the justices want to tighten the legal test for contempt, Epic asks them to send the case back so lower courts can apply the new standard. As [AppDook points out](https://appdook.com/news/epic-supreme-court-brief-apple-contempt), Apple isn't challenging the district court's factual findings, so Epic would be arguing from the same facts on remand.

## What happens next

Apple's reply brief is due **December 14**. Arguments follow on January 11, with a decision likely before the Court's term ends next summer. In the meantime, a separate fight continues in district court over what "reasonable" fee Apple could charge on links. That fee only matters if the contempt ruling survives.

## Our take

Strip away the legal language and this is a case about one button: the "buy on our website" link inside an app. Right now in the U.S., developers can offer it without paying Apple anything, which gives them room to charge less on the web. If Apple wins, [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/09/14/apple-supreme-court-contempt-ruling/) expects the App Store to snap back to its pre-2025 rules, and that reason to offer a cheaper web price mostly goes away.

Epic's brief is strong on the facts, and Apple isn't really disputing them. Apple's bet is that the Court cares more about the wording of a 75-word order than about what Apple did to work around it. That's a real legal argument, but it's a tough one to make when the record says Apple set the fee so the links wouldn't get used.

If an app you pay for offers a web checkout, it's worth comparing prices before you renew. Whether that option is still around next summer depends on January 11.
