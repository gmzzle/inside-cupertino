Apple's replacing the iPhone Upgrade Program with something called Apple Upgrade, and [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/07/25/top-stories-apple-upgrade-program/) reports it launches July 28 with Klarna handling the backend. The new program covers iPhones, iPads, Macs, and Apple Watches with 24-month terms for phones and watches, 36-month terms for tablets and computers.

This is objectively worse than what iPhone buyers have now. The current iPhone Upgrade Program is 24 months with AppleCare+ baked in and a guaranteed upgrade path at 12 months if you want it. The new program? No mention of included AppleCare, and while you can upgrade early, the terms aren't clear on whether that's at 12 months or some other arbitrary point Klarna decides. Partnering with a buy-now-pay-later company known for aggressive lending practices is also a choice.

The expansion to other product categories makes sense on paper - Apple's been pushing services revenue hard, and getting people on payment plans keeps them in the ecosystem. But 36 months for a Mac is a long time in tech years. That's three annual upgrade cycles where you're still paying off last generation's hardware. And unlike the iPhone program where you're basically renting with an escape hatch, a three-year Mac lease feels like indentured servitude to a computer that'll be two chips behind before you're done paying.

This comes right after Apple raised Apple Music to $11.99 and bumped Mac and iPad prices last month. The pattern is clear: tighter monetization across the board. Monthly revenue is more predictable than one-time hardware sales, and if they can normalize the idea that you never actually own your devices, just endlessly lease them, that's a business model shareholders love.

Apple's betting that convenience beats ownership, and for a lot of people that's probably true. But calling this an upgrade program when it's really just longer financing with a Klarna logo is the kind of semantic game that makes you wonder who exactly is being upgraded here.
