When a company lays off people on a struggling product, the easy read is that it's cutting its losses. Apple's recent Siri layoffs don't fit that story, and the reason they don't says more about Siri's future than the cuts themselves.

According to Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, as [reported by MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/22/siri-layoffs-spared-newer-ai-systems/), the layoffs hit employees working on Siri's older infrastructure, not the newer AI systems. In other words, Apple trimmed the old plumbing and kept the team building what comes next. That's housekeeping, not a retreat.

The harder problem is one no reorg fixes quickly: the calendar.

## The real constraint is the release schedule

In his Sunday "Power On" newsletter, Gurman said employees haven't noticed a major change in how Apple works, despite efforts to speed up development and spread big releases throughout the year.

Here's why that matters. Inside Craig Federighi's software engineering organization, Apple reportedly follows an internal release calendar labeled A through G, with engineering work tied to specific software versions and launch windows. Depending on the release, the roadmap and features for a major OS update are largely set by late summer or early fall of the year before it ships.

Think about what that means in practice. The features in next fall's big iPhone update were mostly decided around the time this year's update was shipping. Meanwhile, rival AI developers update their models and services far more often than once a year. Gurman argues this structure leaves Apple with little room to react when a competitor makes a breakthrough in the middle of a development cycle.

That's the core issue. Apple's annual rhythm works well for hardware-linked features, design changes, and anything that needs years of polish. It works poorly for a product category where the leaders can ship something meaningfully better in a matter of months. You can't out-iterate a monthly release cadence with a yearly one, no matter how many engineers you hire or let go.

## What's still wrong with Siri today

Gurman also listed the problems people actually notice day to day: slow responses, limited third-party integration, and dictation that seems to be getting worse.

None of those are about headcount on old infrastructure. They're about how fast Apple can ship fixes and how much of the app world Siri can reach. Dictation in particular is something people use constantly, so any slide there is felt right away, and it's the kind of thing that ideally gets fixed in weeks, not in next year's release.

## What to expect in December

The near-term news is real, if narrow. Siri's multistep control of Apple's own apps is expected to launch in December. That means chaining actions across Apple's built-in apps in a single request, rather than doing one thing at a time.

Set your expectations to match:

- **It's Apple apps first.** The December rollout is about Apple's own apps, not the third-party apps you use for banking, food delivery, or messaging.
- **It won't fix speed or dictation by itself.** Multistep control is a new capability, not a performance overhaul. Treat any improvement in response times as a bonus.
- **It's a step, not the agent.** This is not the more advanced AI agent Apple is reportedly building.

## What waits until 2027

Two bigger items sit further out:

1. **Third-party app support** for Siri's multistep control is expected in 2027. Developers already have early tools. Apple opened a [Siri AI Developer Preview](/articles/siri-ai-open-to-all-developers-app-intents-f1831ca3) in the iOS 27.3 betas, with App Store submissions opening in the spring. But for most iPhone owners, the apps they rely on won't be fully wired in until next year.
2. **A more advanced Apple AI agent** is in development, but Gurman doesn't expect it before late 2027.

That second date is the one to watch. Gurman warned that rival agents could become hard to give up as they build up users' preferences and connected accounts. The longer people spend teaching another assistant how they live and work, the higher the bar Apple has to clear to win them back.

Apple does have real advantages. Its privacy reputation and deeper access to its own platform could help it compete. That platform access is a double-edged sword, though: as we covered this morning, [Signal just walled off Siri AI on iPhone](/articles/signal-block-siri-ai-screenshots-iphone-82ce2ead) because it doesn't trust what system-level AI can see. Deep access only helps if apps and users are comfortable granting it.

## The bottom line

The layoffs aren't the story. They reportedly spared the new AI work, which is what you'd want if you're hoping Siri gets better.

The story is that Apple is trying to compete in a fast-moving field on a schedule where most of next year's features are locked a year in advance. Until that changes, Siri improvements will keep arriving in a few big, predictable drops instead of steady upgrades.

For iPhone owners, the practical plan is simple. Try multistep control with Apple's apps when it lands in December. Don't expect your third-party apps to join until 2027. And don't expect Apple's answer to the newest rival AI agents until late 2027 at the earliest.
