[Six Colors](https://sixcolors.com/sponsor/2026/10/filmlog-for-the-love-of-movies-now-on-the-mac-2/) flagged Filmlog's Mac debut this week, and it's worth noting mostly because this is how indie software used to work: one person builds something they want, charges a fair price, and sticks with it for over a decade without pivoting to surveillance capitalism.

Filmlog has been iPhone and iPad-only since 2013. Now it's a proper Mac app too, which means you can finally maintain your movie diary without squinting at your phone or awkwardly using your iPad as a laptop. There's also an Apple TV companion for managing your watchlist, which is the kind of platform reach that makes sense when you're not reinventing your business model every 18 months.

What's refreshing here is the business model. It's paid up front. No ads, no subscription treadmill, no "but we need to monetize your viewing habits." This is the app economy Apple pretends still exists in keynotes but rarely promotes in practice. The App Store's algorithms have spent years pushing free-to-start, subscription-laden apps that treat users like retention metrics. Meanwhile, apps like Filmlog just exist as tools you buy and use.

The timing is also interesting. We're supposedly in a Mac renaissance—Apple keeps telling us so, anyway—but the Mac App Store remains a weird ghost town where discovery is broken and most developers would rather sell direct. Filmlog shipping a native Mac version in 2026 is either optimism or evidence that Catalyst and SwiftUI finally make cross-platform development tolerable enough to bother.

Filmlog isn't going to replace Letterboxd for the social-film-nerd crowd, but that's sort of the point. It's personal and offline by design, which means your movie taste isn't getting packaged into an engagement graph for venture capitalists to discuss. For an app built by one person over 11 years, that kind of restraint is either admirable or financially impossible, depending on how cynical you are about indie software's future.
