Ron Johnson, the guy who built Apple's retail operation from scratch and made it the most profitable per-square-foot business in the industry, has written a book called *Shop Different: How Retail Revealed Apple's Genius*. He appeared on [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/17/9to5mac-overtime-082-origins-of-the-apple-store-w-special-guest-ron-johnson/)'s Overtime podcast to discuss it, and the whole thing sounds like the kind of insider account people have been waiting two decades to read.

The Apple Store story is genuinely fascinating if you weren't around for it. In 2001, Gateway was closing stores, Dell was mall-kiosk only, and the idea of Apple—then maybe 3% market share—opening branded retail felt like expensive suicide. Johnson convinced Steve Jobs otherwise, and they prototyped the whole thing in a warehouse in Cupertino before opening store number one in Tysons Corner. The Genius Bar, the open floor plan, the long tables—none of that existed in tech retail. It worked so well that by the time the iPhone launched in 2007, Apple had built-in launch day theater at hundreds of locations. No carrier store could touch that.

Then Johnson left in 2011 to become CEO of JCPenney, and it went catastrophically. He tried to transplant Apple's no-sales, no-coupons pricing strategy to a department store chain whose entire customer base ran on coupons, and revenue cratered 25% in a year. He was out in 17 months. It's the kind of failure that defines a career in a different way, and the fact that he's willing to talk about it in the book is probably the most interesting part. Most execs would bury that chapter.

Apple retail today is in a weird spot. The stores still look great and move absurd amounts of product, but they've lost some of the original mission. The Genius Bar is appointment-only and often overwhelmed. The in-store experience for learning how to use your devices has been deprioritized in favor of pushing accessories and services. Johnson's take on where it stands now—especially compared to what he and Jobs built—is worth hearing, even if he's obviously not going to torch the operation he created.

The book comes out this fall, and if it's as candid as the podcast sounds, it'll be required reading for anyone interested in how Apple actually works when the product isn't enough.
