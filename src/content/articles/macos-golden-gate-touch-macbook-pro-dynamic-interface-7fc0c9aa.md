Three days out from Apple's "Pro and tell" experience in Los Angeles, the hardware is mostly a known quantity. The open question has been the software: how do you put a touchscreen on a Mac without turning it into an iPad?

Bloomberg's Mark Gurman offered the clearest answer yet in his Power On newsletter this morning. As [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/25/macbook-pro-with-touchscreen-on-wednesday/) summarized it, Apple plans to unveil 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro models with touch-enabled OLED displays on **Wednesday, October 28**, and macOS Golden Gate will ship with what Gurman calls a "dynamic interface" for touch.

## The hardware, briefly

We [laid out the expected lineup](/articles/apple-pro-and-tell-october-28-touchscreen-macbook-pro-ef11fa66/) when invitations went out last Monday, and Gurman's latest report doesn't change much. Per MacRumors, the new models:

- **Look a lot like today's MacBook Pro**, but weigh less. (We covered the "significantly lighter" report [here](/articles/macbook-pro-redesign-significantly-lighter-bf2c87eb/).)
- **Swap the notch for a Dynamic Island** at the top of the display.
- **Run the M5 Pro and M5 Max chips** Apple already released in March, not new silicon.

The format is still a press release at 10 a.m. Pacific followed by hands-on time for invited media, not a keynote.

## How touch is supposed to work

This is the new part. Gurman, as quoted by MacRumors, says the system "will take previous interactions into account, presenting controls based on whether someone has been touching the display or using the trackpad." He adds that "touching a button can also bring up a menu around the user's finger, while the Dock and Menu Bar have been optimized for touch."

In plain terms, macOS would watch how you've been using it. Reach up and tap, and controls get friendlier to a fingertip. Go back to the trackpad, and the interface presumably settles back into its familiar, denser layout.

Apple will pitch touch as an *additional* input method, used when it makes sense, alongside the keyboard and trackpad, Gurman said. [The Mac Observer](https://www.macobserver.com/news/apple-executives-wanted-a-touchscreen-macbook-pro-but-designers-pushed-back/), also citing Gurman, describes that as a compromise. It reports that Tim Cook and John Ternus came to believe customers expected touch on a laptop, while Apple's industrial design team pushed back. The result, per that report: scrolling, swiping, pinching and zooming on the display, with the keyboard and trackpad still the main way you drive the Mac.

That framing matters. Apple has spent more than a decade arguing against touchscreen laptops, going back to Steve Jobs' 2010 critique of vertical touchscreens that The Mac Observer recalls. A Mac that quietly adapts, rather than one that asks you to change how you work, is the version of this reversal Apple can most easily defend.

## The price question

OLED panels cost more, and buyers should expect to pay for them. MacRumors notes that today's mini-LED MacBook Pro with M5 Pro starts at **$2,499** for the 14-inch and **$2,999** for the 16-inch in the U.S., and says starting prices for the new models could be up to **$500 higher**.

[9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/10/25/macbook-pro-redesign-oled-touch-launching-this-week/) sketched how the lineup could shake out. It's labeled as guesswork, not reporting:

- **Entry 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6:** current design, likely around $1,999
- **Current mini-LED 14- and 16-inch (M5 Pro/M5 Max):** unchanged at $2,499 and $2,999
- **New OLED touch 14- and 16-inch (M5 Pro/M5 Max):** 9to5Mac guesses $2,999 and $3,499, and says they could start higher

The detail that makes that three-tier picture plausible: Gurman says the existing mini-LED models will **stay on sale for now** before eventually being discontinued. 9to5Mac adds that, per research firm Omdia, the mini-LED panels for MacBook Pro remain in production through 2028.

## What this means

- **You won't be forced into touch.** If Gurman is right, the current mini-LED MacBook Pro sticks around, so pro users who keep their laptops docked to external displays can skip the premium.
- **Same chips, different reason to upgrade.** With M5 Pro and M5 Max carrying over, the new models are a display, design and input upgrade, not a performance jump. Anyone who bought an M5 Pro or M5 Max MacBook Pro this year isn't missing out on speed.
- **The software is the real test.** An adaptive interface sounds elegant in a newsletter. Whether it feels natural, or just inconsistent, is something only hands-on time will tell. Third-party apps adapting to touch is another unknown, and the iPhone Duo's launch showed how long that can take.
- **Watch the sticker.** Pricing will decide whether this is a mainstream upgrade or a halo product. Nothing is official until Apple publishes it.

Apple hasn't confirmed any of this. We'll have the official specs, prices and release date when the press releases land Wednesday morning.
