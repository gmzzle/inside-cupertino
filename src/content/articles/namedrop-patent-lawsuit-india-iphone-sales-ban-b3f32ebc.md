"iPhone sales ban" is a headline built to make you look twice. Read past it and this case is about one small feature most people forget they have, and about a startup that wants to get paid for it.

The feature is NameDrop, the iOS 17 trick where you hold two iPhones close together (or an iPhone and an Apple Watch) and swap contact cards. The plaintiff is GG Technologies, the Santa Monica company behind the contact-sharing app StayTouch. According to [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/startup-sues-apple-in-india-over-tap-to-share-contacts-feature/), which reviewed the court documents, it has sued Apple in the Bombay High Court and is seeking ₹15 billion, or about $162 million, in damages plus an injunction.

## What the startup is actually asking for

The claim is that NameDrop infringes GG Technologies' Indian patent on letting nearby phones exchange contact details without manual pairing. TechCrunch reports the filing runs 781 pages. The company told the outlet it filed in October after mediation that began in April broke down in September.

The part that made headlines is the remedy. The startup wants the court to stop Apple from selling or importing iPhones and Apple Watches in India that come with NameDrop preloaded. Its own framing is narrower than the headlines, though. "We are seeking an injunction to restrict the usage of the NameDrop feature which is native to iOS," GG Technologies told TechCrunch.

That sentence is the key. The target is a feature, not the phone.

Some context on timing:

- StayTouch launched in 2019. GG Technologies applied for its Indian patent in 2021 and got it in July 2024, per TechCrunch and [The Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/iit-delhi-alumnus-startup-sues-apple-for-rs-1500-crore-over-namedrop-feature/articleshow/136074433.cms).
- Apple announced NameDrop in June 2023, so the feature shipped before that Indian patent was granted.
- The case hasn't been listed for a hearing yet. Apple didn't respond to TechCrunch's request for comment.

## This isn't the only front

India is one piece of a wider campaign. GG Technologies also sued Apple in the U.S. in April, and the Times of India reports the company says it filed a second U.S. suit in November. In August, the U.S. International Trade Commission opened an investigation after the startup's complaint, and the company wants an order that could block imports of infringing Apple devices, as [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/23/iphone-sales-ban-namedrop-feature/) notes. Opening an ITC investigation isn't a ruling on the merits, and the startup itself acknowledges no final finding on infringement has been made.

Filing in several places at once is a familiar playbook. Each venue that could, at least in theory, block sales adds pressure to settle or license. The size of the damages claim and the scope of the injunction are opening positions, not a forecast.

## Why a sales ban is the unlikely outcome

Apple has been through this before. When the ITC ruled against it in the Masimo blood-oxygen dispute, Apple didn't stop selling Apple Watches in the U.S. It turned off the contested feature and kept shipping. A contact-swapping shortcut is far easier to give up than a health sensor.

If a court ever sided with GG Technologies, the most realistic outcomes are a licensing deal, a settlement, a redesign of how NameDrop detects and pairs devices, or NameDrop going dark on Indian iPhones. Pulling every iPhone and Apple Watch from one of Apple's most important growth markets over a feature this minor would be a strange business call. India is also a major iPhone manufacturing base, and TechCrunch points out iPhones are still under 10% of smartphone shipments there, so Apple has plenty of room to grow and plenty of reason to make this go away quietly.

There's a familiar undertone too. [The Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/technology/tech-news-technology/apple-namedrop-feature-lawsuit-india-10973502/) frames the dispute in terms of "sherlocking," the long-running complaint that Apple builds features that make small developers' apps redundant. Whether that's what happened here is for the court to decide. But the pattern is why cases like this get sympathy even when the requested remedy sounds extreme.

## What it means if you own an iPhone

Nothing changes today. No hearing is scheduled, nothing has been decided, and NameDrop works the same as it did last week. If you live in India and were planning to buy an iPhone or Apple Watch, this isn't a reason to rush or wait.

If you've never used NameDrop and would rather it not exist, you don't need a court order. [Apple says](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/namedrop-iphone-share-contact-info-iph1b6c664b7/ios) NameDrop is on by default, and you can turn it off at Settings > General > AirDrop by switching off Bringing Devices Together. That also turns off the other bring-devices-together features, so flip it back on if you miss them.

The thing to watch is whether this ends quietly. A licensing deal or settlement would never touch your phone. A real court loss would most likely mean a feature quietly vanishing, not empty shelves.
