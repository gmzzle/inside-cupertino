Elevation Lab has pulled the Silencer, its $9.99 tool for permanently killing an AirTag's speaker. In a short post titled ["An apology"](https://www.elevationlab.com/blogs/news/an-apology), signed by CEO Casey Hopkins, the company said today that it built the tool for AirTags hidden on its own bikes, and that it missed what else it does:

> In retrospect though, this also has the side effect of aiding someone with bad intentions to track someone that wouldn't get phone notifications. We have removed the product and will use our patent on it to prevent anyone else from making one in the future.

The product page now returns a 404. [MacRumors reports](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/13/elevation-lab-airtag-silencer/) the company also emailed customers to say it's no longer for sale. MacRumors also said it had deliberately not covered the Silencer's launch, so it wouldn't publicize an accessory that strips out an AirTag anti-stalking feature.

## What the Silencer did

The Silencer was a small steel jig with a hardened pin. Elevation Lab's instructions were to take the battery out of the AirTag, set it on the baseplate, put the Silencer on top, and hit the pin flush with a hammer so it punched through to the speaker. If the tag still made noise, you rotated it and hit it again. It worked on both the original AirTag and AirTag 2, and the listing warned it "may void AirTag's warranty."

The pitch was theft recovery. An AirTag that's been away from its owner for a while chirps when it's moved, which can tip off whoever stole your bike, bag or tools.

## Why the speaker matters

That chirp isn't a bug. It's one half of Apple's defense against people using AirTags to track someone without their knowledge. [Six Colors' Glenn Fleishman](https://sixcolors.com/post/2026/10/silencing-airtags-enables-stalkers-and-domestic-violence/), who called the Silencer "appalling" when it launched, laid out how it works. Since mid-2021, an AirTag separated from its owner plays a sound when moved, at a random point between 8 and 24 hours after its paired device was last in Bluetooth range. Separately, iPhones and Android phones warn you when an unknown tracker is moving with you, show you a map of where it has followed you, and let you play a sound to find it. With the speaker gone, that last step doesn't work either.

Apple went the other way with the AirTag 2 this year: Fleishman notes its speaker is 50 percent louder, and MacRumors says it uses more adhesive to make the speaker harder to pull out.

When Fleishman asked Hopkins about misuse earlier this month, he defended the product by email: "If someone is determined enough, they could already silence it themselves, but the person would still get phone notifications." Eva Galperin, director of cybersecurity at the Electronic Frontier Foundation, told Six Colors at the time that "you cannot make a tracker that does not alert a thief without also making a person a tool for stalking."

Today's apology quietly concedes the gap in Hopkins' original argument. Phone alerts only help people who carry a phone that gets them. For anyone who doesn't, the chirp is the only warning there is.

## Our take

Good call, and a fast one. Elevation Lab makes some of the best AirTag mounts around, and its reasoning for the Silencer wasn't sinister. But a precision tool that turns disabling the safety speaker into a hammer tap or two is different from a YouTube tutorial, and the company's promise to use its patent to block copycats is a better outcome than a quiet delisting.

The real tension isn't going away. A tracker that stays silent for theft recovery is the same tracker a stalker wants, and Apple has picked a side. If you've hidden an AirTag on a bike, that means living with the chirp. If your phone ever warns you that an unknown AirTag is traveling with you, take it seriously. The alert shows where it has followed you, and you can disable the tag by twisting off the back and removing the battery.
